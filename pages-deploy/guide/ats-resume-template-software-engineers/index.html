ATS-Friendly Resume Template for Software Engineers (2026)

CheckATS · Updated October 14, 2026

You do not need a designer to get past an applicant tracking system — you need a deliberately boring template. This guide gives software engineers an ATS-friendly resume template for 2026: the sections to include, the order to put them in, and how to verify that the text the software extracts matches the file your recruiter sees. No template guarantees an interview, but a clean one removes formatting as a reason to fail.

The short answer: an ATS-friendly resume template for software engineers is a single-column, text-based layout with standard section headers (Summary, Skills, Experience, Education), exact technology names in a dedicated Skills section, and a selectable-text PDF export. Skip graphics, tables, and columns — then verify the result by checking the extracted text yourself.

What does an ATS-friendly resume template look like?

The answer: it looks boring — one column of plain text with your name and contact info at the top, then Summary, Skills, Experience, and Education under standard headers. No tables, no graphics, no columns, no unusual fonts. Every parser reads this layout the same way: top to bottom, line by line.

The full skeleton — copy it into any text editor and it already works. The point of the template is that there is nothing clever in it for a parser to get wrong:

YOUR NAME
email · phone · city · linkedin.com/in/you · github.com/you

SUMMARY
Software engineer with X years of experience in <stack>.
Shipped <result with a number>. Previously at <company>.

SKILLS
Languages: Go, Python, TypeScript
Frameworks: React, FastAPI, gRPC
Cloud & DevOps: AWS (EC2, S3, Lambda), Docker, Kubernetes, Terraform
Databases: PostgreSQL, Redis, Elasticsearch
Tools: Git, Linux, GitHub Actions

EXPERIENCE
Senior Software Engineer — Company A, City | 2023-01 – Present
- Built <system> serving <X> daily users; reduced p99 latency by <Y>%
- <verb> <task>; <quantified result>

Software Engineer — Company B, City | 2021-06 – 2023-01
- <verb> <task>; <quantified result>

PROJECTS (optional)
Project name — one line on the stack, one line on the result.

EDUCATION
B.S. Computer Science — University | 2017-09 – 2021-06

Notice what is missing: no sidebar, no skill bars, no photo, no icons, no header or footer tricks. Each of those asks the parser to guess. The template also fixes keyword dilution: exact technology names near the top in the Skills section let a keyword scan find them immediately. Generic soft-skill words like "team player" do not belong here — CheckATS deliberately excludes them from keyword counting, since they match everything and signal nothing.

How should I fill in each section of the template?

The answer: put contact info on separate plain-text lines; write a two-to-three-line summary naming your stack; list exact technology names in Skills, spelled exactly as job posts spell them; write experience bullets as verb plus task plus quantified result; and keep dates in one consistent month-year format. Accuracy matters more than volume — never claim a skill you do not have.

Contact. Email and phone each get their own line in plain text — not in a page header, graphic, or icon, since some extractors read headers separately and a missing email is the most common mechanical failure. Include your city, your LinkedIn URL, and your GitHub URL, where no parser can miss them.

Summary. Two to three lines with one job to do: name your years of experience, your core stack, and one quantified result. "Backend engineer with 4 years in Go and Python; built billing services processing $2M monthly; ex-Stripe." If you cannot fill three lines honestly, write two — a padded summary is worse than a short one.

Skills. This is the highest-density keyword area of your resume. List exact technology names — languages, frameworks, cloud platforms, tools — spelled exactly as job posts spell them. When the job description says "Kubernetes," write "Kubernetes," not just "K8s": CheckATS uses case-insensitive exact matching, so "K8s" does not match "Kubernetes" in this tool. Other systems do recognize related terms (Greenhouse Talent Matching explicitly supports that), but the conservative move is to include both the acronym and the full term. Group skills into labeled lines — Languages, Frameworks, Cloud & DevOps, Databases — so a human can scan them in seconds.

Experience. Reverse chronological, one consistent date format (this template uses 2023-01; CheckATS also recognizes 2023.01, 01/2023, and Chinese year/month dates, but not "Jan 2023"). Each bullet follows the same shape: strong verb, what you built or changed, and a number — "reduced p99 latency by 40%," "led migration serving 2M daily users." Use only figures you can substantiate in an interview: a fabricated metric that collapses under one follow-up question costs you the interview.

Projects. Optional, but for engineers with open-source work or side projects, one line on the stack and one line on the result is enough — with a link to the repository. Project technologies feed the same exact-term matching as the Skills section.

Education. Degree, school, dates. Keep it at the bottom unless you are a new graduate, in which case it moves above Experience.

Which template features break ATS parsing?

The answer: multi-column layouts, tables used for structure, text inside graphics or icons, contact info hidden in headers or footers, uncommon fonts with custom encoding, and scanned-image PDFs. None of these fails every parser — but each one scrambles reading order or hides text from an extractor, so the conservative choice is to avoid all of them.

Two columns. The most common offender. Some parsers read left column then right; others read straight across the row, stitching unrelated sentences together. You cannot control which one your employer's system uses.

Tables. Tables used purely for visual alignment (name left, dates right) often extract as a jumbled sequence. Use tabs or spacing instead — alignment that is plain text underneath.

Graphics, icons, and text in images. Skill bars, star ratings, and icon-based contact info are invisible to text extractors. If the information matters, it must exist as selectable text. That includes profile photos: nothing a parser can read, and in many markets bias risk for no benefit.

Headers and footers. Some parsers treat header/footer content as metadata and strip it; others repeat it on every page. Contact info placed there can vanish or duplicate — keep it in the body of the document.

Scanned or image-based PDFs. If you cannot select and copy the text, neither can the parser. CheckATS evaluates extracted text and imports text-based PDFs and pasted text; it does not run OCR on scanned PDFs, so a scanned resume is unreadable to this tool regardless of how good the template was.

How do I know my template actually works?

The answer: do the copy-paste test — select all text in your PDF, paste it into a plain text file, and confirm nothing is missing and the reading order is correct. Then run an ATS check with a real job description: CheckATS scores JD exact-term keyword coverage at 60% and plain-text parseability (contact info, consistent dates, no garbled text, reasonable length) at 40%.

The copy-paste test takes ten seconds and catches most formatting failures: missing contact lines, scrambled column order, garbled characters from an unusual font. If the pasted text reads like a coherent document, your template's structure is fine.

The ATS check tests the other half: relevance. Paste your resume and the actual job description into the checker and look at which must-have keywords are missing. A low keyword score usually means a wording problem — the JD says "Kubernetes" and you only wrote "K8s" — not a template problem. CheckATS counts what you wrote, exactly as you wrote it, by design.

Two honest caveats before you trust any score. First, the score is a simulation, not a prediction: CheckATS cannot see the employer's screening rules, so its number is not your probability of passing. Second, keyword coverage is only half the picture — the four-dimension AI review (wording strength, quantification and results, specificity of experience, job fit) covers how a human reader will judge the same page. After an AI rewrite, the word-level diff shows exactly what changed, so you keep what is true and drop what is not.

Frequently asked questions

Is a PDF or a DOCX safer for an ATS-friendly resume?

Follow the employer's instructions first. When you get a choice, a text-based PDF whose text you can select and copy is a safe default. CheckATS imports text-based PDFs and pasted text; it does not import DOCX files or run OCR on scanned PDFs.

Do I need a different template for each job application?

No. Keep the same template structure and tailor the content: adjust the Skills emphasis and reword two or three bullets per job description. The template is the container; the keywords and wording inside it are what you change per application.

Is it true that 75% of resumes are automatically rejected by ATS?

No reliable evidence supports that figure — it comes from a 2012 marketing claim that has been repeated ever since. The only real automatic rejections are knockout questions (such as work authorization). Real ATS products do parsing plus keyword matching, and there is no universal ATS score across employers.

Should my ATS-friendly resume be one page or two?

One page is the norm for early-career engineers; two pages is fine once you have roughly eight to ten years of relevant experience. Page count is not what parsers care about — clean extracted text is. Keep it concise enough that a recruiter can scan it in under a minute.
